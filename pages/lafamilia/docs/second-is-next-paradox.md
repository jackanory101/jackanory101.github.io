# Second is next paradox

## 1. The Literal Logic Problem ("Next" Means "Nearest")

In almost every other context in the English language, the word **"next"** means *nearest in time, sequence, or space*:

  

- **"The next train"** = The very next train arriving, not the second one.

    

- **"The next house"** = The house immediately adjacent, not two doors down.

    

- **"Next in line"** = The person immediately following the current one.

    

When applied to days of the week, literal logic dictates that **"next Thursday"** must mean *the first Thursday encountered as you move forward in time*.

  

By overriding the literal meaning of "next" and assigning it to mean *"the second occurrence from now"*, the idiom forces the speaker to bypass the actual next item in the sequence. It creates a strange paradox where the "next" Thursday isn't actually the next Thursday you reach.

  

## 2. The Unclear Boundary Problem (Where does "This" end?)

The idiom relies on the idea of a **"current week"**, but human perception of time doesn't naturally align with arbitrary calendar boundaries (like Sunday or Monday night).

  

This creates severe edge cases depending on when the sentence is spoken:

  

- **Spoken on a Monday:** "This Thursday" is 3 days away; "Next Thursday" is 10 days away. (Relatively clear under the idiom's rules).

    

- **Spoken on a Wednesday:** "This Thursday" is tomorrow. "Next Thursday" is 8 days away.

    

- **Spoken on a Friday:** "This Thursday" was yesterday! So if someone says "next Thursday," do they mean in 6 days (the next one on the timeline) or in 13 days (the Thursday of the *following* week)?

    

Because the reference point shifts continuously throughout the week, the definition of "this" vs. "next" constantly breaks down.

  

## 3. Structural Overhead and Double-Handling

An efficient communication system minimizes ambiguity. This idiom does the exact opposite:

  

1.  **It overcomplicates simple concepts:** Instead of having one word for the nearest occurrence ("Thursday") and a modifier for the subsequent one ("Thursday week" or "the Thursday after"), it forces two ambiguous terms ("this" and "next") to compete for the same immediate slot.

      

2.  **It requires immediate meta-communication:** Because people intuitively sense the ambiguity, using the phrase almost always requires an immediate verbal disclaimer:

      

    > *"Let's meet next Thursday... as in, not this coming one, but the week after."*
    >
    >   

If a phrase regularly requires a follow-up sentence to explain what it actually means, the original phrase has failed as a tool of logic.

  

## Summary

| **Logic Type**                            | **Phrase**      | **Target Day**                                                     |
|-------------------------------------------|-----------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **Sequential Logic** *(Standard English)* | "Next Thursday" | The very first Thursday you arrive at on the calendar.             |
| **Idiomatic Logic** *(Australian/WA)*     | "Next Thursday" | Skip the first Thursday; go to the Thursday of the following week. |

It is problematic because it pits **positional sequence** (1st vs 2nd) against **calendar grouping** (this week vs next week), leaving the listener to guess which framework the speaker is using.
