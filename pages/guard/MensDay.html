Should there be an International Men’s Day?

See MensDay.pdf

Mon 10 Aug 2026

Summary

These sources examine the debate surrounding International Men’s Day by contrasting two opposing viewpoints and providing a rhetorical analysis of their arguments. Oscar Rickett argues against the necessity of the day, suggesting that it is a dismissive reaction to women’s progress and that men already hold dominant social power. Conversely, Glen Poole advocates for the initiative, highlighting specific vulnerabilities of men and boys, such as high suicide rates and educational underperformance, which are often overlooked. The included exegesis critiques the analytical weaknesses in Rickett’s approach, specifically his use of ridicule and the conflation of all men with those in high-level positions of power. Ultimately, the text illustrates a fundamental conflict between viewing gender through a binary lens of power versus a multifaceted framework of individual and group-specific outcomes. This comparison reveals how editorial framing and moral language can shape the perceived legitimacy of gender-based advocacy.

Oscar Rickett and Glen Poole

Is this day a frightened reaction to an upturn in women’s fortunes or a vital initiative to help improve the lives of men and boys?

Tue 19 Nov 2013 20.59 AEDT

Oscar Rickett: ‘Every day is men’s day, despite Angela Merkel’

On the International Men’s Day website, true believers from around the world look into the camera, normally with the surf breaking on a beach behind them (men like being outdoors), and tell us about men and boys, health and role models. They don’t come across as secret woman-oppressors or evil patriarchy peddlers. They’re just guys who like to say “men” and variations on the word “men” a lot. There is a cult-like feeling that is more reminiscent of the Scouts - whose founder Robert Baden-Powell they quote admiringly - than it is of the Masons. There is a moderately creepy photo of a boy in a suit that’s too big for him but the main thrust seems to be that this is a group that wants to deal with men’s health problems and promote gender equality - both admirable aims.

The problem, though, is that a bunch of men gathered round the barbecue - one of the website’s suggestions for guaranteed IMD fun - might have quite different ideas about gender equality to a bunch of women gathered around the sewing machine (is that what women like to do these days?) It calls to mind a delegation of men trooping back to their women folk, proudly announcing that they have agreed upon a set of actions that the women will surely accept, despite having no input into. The website even talks about “honour”, as if we should all aspire to be Achilles or Hector, while our womenfolk worry and wait for the inevitable bereavement.

This day, then, is a frightened reaction to the perceived upturn in women’s fortunes as well as being simply another way of establishing the gender power structure that has existed in almost all cultures since the dawn of time. That power structure is this: men have most of the power. This remains the same, despite Angela Merkel. Every single day is international men’s day because day after day, in country after country, far more men wield far more power than their female counterparts. The conversation about equality is one that needs to involve as many people as possible. It can’t be carried out by a group of men alone, however well-meaning and good at surfing they are.

Oscar Rickett is a freelance writer, researcher and sometime actor

Glen Poole: ‘We can’t ignore the problems faced by men and boys’

International Men’s Day is an important date for anyone who is concerned with equality.

We all care about equality for different reasons. We know that the world’s most equal countries tend to be happier, healthier, safer places for everyone to live in. So whether you’re rich, poor, straight, gay, female, male or any other gender identity you can imagine, if the country you live in is unequal, you will feel the impact of that inequality in some way.

When it comes to gender equality in particular, we still tend to think of it is a one-sided, binary puzzle in which women have problems and men are the problem - and so our solutions generally focus on helping women and girls and fixing men and boys.

In reality, gender equality is a much more complex and multi-faceted beast in which people of all genders can experience a broad range of different inequalities.

When it comes to men and boys in particular, we know that in 99% of countries men die sooner than women; that men are at greater risk of suicide than women; that in nearly 100 countries girls are outperforming boys in school; that men and boys account for more than 80% of violent deaths worldwide every year and that fathers all over the globe are less involved in raising their children than mothers for all sorts of personal, cultural and political reasons.

There are many inequalities that disproportionately affect men and boys. We cannot and should not expect feminism and the women’s movement to address these aspects of gender equality. Initiatives like International Men’s Day are vitally important as they provide us with a global platform where people from a diverse range of perspectives who are committed to improving the lives of men and boys can get involved in the fight for equality.

Of course we could try creating a more gender-equal world by only focusing on the problems that women and girls face. But it seems self evident that things would be in much better shape if we were taking effective action to tackle the problems that men and boys face too.

Glen Poole is director of the consultancy Helping Men, UK co-ordinator for International Men’s Day and author of the book Equality For Men

Yes. This is a particularly interesting article because it contains two radically different rhetorical treatments of men’s issues. Rickett’s half is quite hostile and dismissive; Poole’s half is an attempt to correct exactly that framing. In fact, it gives us a useful comparison within the same article.

Exegesis

1. Rickett begins with ridicule rather than argument

The opening description is loaded:

“true believers from around the world”

“They don’t come across as secret woman-oppressors or evil patriarchy peddlers.”

“cult-like feeling”

“men like being outdoors”

“a moderately creepy photo of a boy”

This is classic delegitimising rhetoric through ridicule. Before the substantive argument begins, the reader is encouraged to regard the people involved as slightly ridiculous, creepy, cultish and ideologically suspect.

Notice that there is no equivalent description of women’s organisations as:

“a bunch of women gathered around the sewing machine”

or similar ridicule in the other direction.

The barbecue/sewing-machine comparison is especially revealing because the author is supposedly discussing gender stereotypes while simultaneously deploying gender stereotypes for comic effect.

2. “Every day is men’s day” is a rhetorical rather than empirical claim

The headline of Rickett’s section:

“Every day is men’s day”

rests on a very particular definition of equality: aggregate institutional power.

His argument is essentially:

Men occupy more positions of power → therefore men already have their “day” → therefore a men’s day is unnecessary.

But that’s a non sequitur.

International Men’s Day isn’t necessarily intended to celebrate people who hold political and economic power. It is generally about issues affecting males, including health, suicide, education, fatherhood and male role models.

Indeed, Poole’s response makes precisely that point.

The fact that men occupy more powerful positions does not logically imply that men’s health problems are adequately addressed.

You could equally argue:

“Most senior political leaders are men, therefore men’s suicide problem isn’t a legitimate equality issue.”

Obviously the conclusion doesn’t follow.

3. The article conflates “men” with “men in positions of power”

This is perhaps its largest analytical weakness.

Rickett says:

“men have most of the power.”

But which men?

The men who dominate the very top of political and economic institutions aren’t necessarily representative of:

  • unemployed men;
  • homeless men;
  • male prisoners;
  • boys failing at school;
  • men dying by suicide;
  • men doing dangerous work;
  • men killed in wars;
  • fathers separated from children.

There’s a fundamental ecological fallacy here:

Some men have disproportionate power

doesn’t mean:

men as a population are advantaged in every relevant dimension.

Poole’s statistics are essentially an attempt to demonstrate this.

4. Poole’s opening sentence is extraordinarily important

He writes:

“we still tend to think of it as a one-sided, binary puzzle in which women have problems and men are the problem.”

That’s almost exactly the phenomenon you’ve been pointing out in the Guardian’s other articles.

It’s an explicit description of the rhetorical frame:

women = victims / men = perpetrators

rather than:

men and women can each experience different advantages and disadvantages.

Poole then says:

“people of all genders can experience a broad range of different inequalities.”

That is a much more symmetrical analytical framework.

5. The statistics in Poole’s section are much more interesting than Rickett’s argument

He gives several empirical claims:

  • men die younger in 99% of countries;
  • men have substantially higher suicide rates;
  • girls outperform boys educationally in many countries;
  • men and boys account for >80% of violent deaths;
  • fathers are less involved in raising children.

Some of these need qualification, and some of the underlying sources are quite old. But the type of argument is much better:

If gender equality means identifying sex-based disadvantages, we have to examine disadvantages affecting males as well as females.

That’s logically coherent.

And crucially, Poole doesn’t need to deny women’s problems to establish men’s problems.

6. Rickett’s argument contains an interesting double standard

He says:

“The conversation about equality is one that needs to involve as many people as possible. It can’t be carried out by a group of men alone…”

That’s superficially unobjectionable.

But apply the same principle consistently and you get an uncomfortable question:

Why doesn’t the existence of women-only organisations invalidate women’s organisations in the same way?

Feminist organisations routinely discuss women’s issues primarily among women. That’s not necessarily illegitimate.

So if the objection is:

“Men shouldn’t be discussing men’s issues without women”

then it needs to be applied consistently.

Otherwise, it looks less like a principle about inclusiveness and more like a special suspicion of male self-organisation.

7. “Frightened reaction to women’s fortunes” is mind-reading

This sentence is particularly weak:

“This day, then, is a frightened reaction to the perceived upturn in women’s fortunes…”

How does Rickett know?

There are perfectly plausible alternative explanations:

  • concern about male suicide;
  • concern about boys’ educational performance;
  • concern about fathers’ relationships with children;
  • concern about male health;
  • concern about male homelessness;
  • concern about male violence victimisation;
  • concern about male role models.

To establish that International Men’s Day is a frightened reaction to women’s progress, he’d need evidence about the motivations of the people creating and supporting it.

Instead he simply asserts their psychological motivation.

That’s exactly the kind of dubious causal attribution we identified in the Trump article.

8. The most revealing sentence may be this one

“This remains the same, despite Angela Merkel.”

Why mention Merkel?

Because she is an obvious counterexample to the proposition that men possess all political power.

But instead of allowing the counterexample to complicate the thesis, Rickett simply absorbs it:

men have most power → Merkel doesn’t change the overall pattern.

That’s perfectly legitimate if he’s making a statistical argument, but it reveals the level at which he’s operating: aggregate sex categories, not individual circumstances.

Poole does essentially the opposite.

He says:

“people of all genders can experience a broad range of different inequalities.”

That’s a move from group-level power to individual/group-specific outcomes.

9. There is also a striking contrast in moral language

Rickett:

  • “frightened”
  • “creepy”
  • “cult-like”
  • “woman-oppressors”
  • “patriarchy peddlers”
  • “our womenfolk”
  • “gender power structure”

Poole:

  • “important”
  • “equality”
  • “complex”
  • “multi-faceted”
  • “inequalities”
  • “improving lives”
  • “effective action”

So the two halves don’t merely disagree on facts.

They have different rhetorical assumptions about whether men’s collective organisation is inherently suspicious.

Rickett’s position is roughly:

Men already possess power, therefore organised men’s advocacy requires suspicion.

Poole’s position is:

Men can be advantaged in some dimensions and disadvantaged in others, therefore men’s advocacy can be legitimate.

The latter is much closer to a genuinely symmetrical conception of equality.

10. And this connects strongly with the Japanese custody article

There is a striking commonality across the pieces you’ve supplied.

In the Japan Times article, the framework is approximately:

mother → potentially vulnerable person

father → potentially dangerous/excluded person

In the Trump article:

woman → victim of male behaviour

man → potential perpetrator / beneficiary of entitlement

In Rickett’s article:

men → holders of social power

women → historically disadvantaged group

In each case, the group assigned structural power receives less presumption of vulnerability or legitimacy.

That’s not necessarily wrong in any individual case. Men really do commit more violent crime; men really do occupy more positions of power; women really are disproportionately victims of some forms of sexual violence.

The problem arises when a group-level statistical truth is converted into a moral description of individuals within that group.

That’s the recurring rhetorical move we’ve been finding.

One particularly useful test

I’d apply a sex-reversal test to Rickett’s opening.

Imagine a Guardian article saying:

“Every day is women’s day, despite [female prime minister]. The problem with a bunch of women gathered around a sewing machine is that they might have quite different ideas about equality…”

and describing women’s advocacy as a:

“frightened reaction to the perceived upturn in men’s fortunes.”

It would immediately sound derogatory and sexist.

That doesn’t automatically prove Rickett’s argument is wrong. But it demonstrates something important:

The article uses stereotypes and contemptuous characterisation of men that would probably be recognised as unacceptable if directed at women.

And that is probably the strongest case you’ve found so far for the broader hypothesis you’re testing: the asymmetry isn’t necessarily in which facts are selected; it is in whose collective identity is treated as legitimate, whose grievances are presumed authentic, and whose motives are subjected to suspicion.

Loaded language

Yes. If we strip away the substantive arguments and look specifically at word choice and rhetorical loading, Rickett’s section is unusually revealing. The language repeatedly tells the reader how to feel about the men being discussed before establishing what they actually believe or do.

Rickett’s section: loaded language

Phrase Rhetorical effect
“true believers” Religious/cult language; implies irrational faith rather than sincere advocacy.
“men like being outdoors” Mocking gender stereotype; trivialises the people being described.
“cult-like feeling” Strongly delegitimising; associates the organisation with irrational or coercive groups.
“creepy photo” Emotional judgement rather than description; primes the reader to distrust the organisation.
“secret woman-oppressors” Sarcastic straw-man framing; implies that male advocates may secretly be misogynists.
“evil patriarchy peddlers” Ridicule and caricature. The word “evil” makes disagreement morally rather than intellectually framed.
“a bunch of men” Diminutive and dismissive; contrasts with more neutral terms such as “an organisation” or “advocates”.
“gathered round the barbecue” Infantilises/mocks the organisation through a stereotypically masculine social image.
“a bunch of women gathered around the sewing machine” Deliberately stereotypical analogy; simultaneously mocks the men’s stereotype while invoking a female stereotype.
“womenfolk” Archaic and patronising; evokes patriarchal social relations.
“proudly announcing” Attributes arrogance without demonstrating it.
“actions that the women will surely accept” Implies male presumption and entitlement; again imputes motive.
“our womenfolk worry and wait” Strongly sarcastic patriarchal imagery.
“frightened reaction” Psychological attribution: asserts that men’s advocacy is motivated by fear.
“perceived upturn in women’s fortunes” Diminishes men’s stated motivations by recasting them as a reaction to women becoming more successful.
“simply another way of establishing the gender power structure” Presupposes that the organisation’s real function is power preservation.
“men have most of the power” Sweeping collective generalisation; converts aggregate patterns into a statement about “men”.
“far more men wield far more power” Repetition of “far more” rhetorically intensifies the proposition.
“can’t be carried out by a group of men alone” Creates an implicit legitimacy problem specifically for male organisation.

The opening paragraph is particularly loaded

Consider the cumulative effect:

“true believers” → “men like being outdoors” → “cult-like feeling” → “creepy” → “secret woman-oppressors” → “evil patriarchy peddlers”

Any one of these could perhaps be defended as humour. Taken together, they establish an overwhelmingly negative frame before the argument has even begun.

The reader isn’t first told:

International Men’s Day advocates say they want to address men’s health, education and fatherhood.

Instead, we’re given a comic portrait of strange men on beaches, with a creepy photograph and vaguely cultish characteristics.

That’s affective framing: the emotional evaluation precedes the factual evaluation.

There is also a striking amount of motive attribution

Some of the most questionable language isn’t insulting; it’s mind-reading.

For example:

“frightened reaction”

“perceived upturn in women’s fortunes”

“another way of establishing the gender power structure”

These aren’t descriptions of observable behaviour. They’re claims about why men are doing what they’re doing.

A more neutral formulation would be:

“Critics argue that International Men’s Day arose partly in response to advances in women’s rights.”

Rickett instead states the interpretation as fact:

“This day, then, is a frightened reaction…”

The word “frightened” is doing substantial argumentative work. It implies that the men’s movement isn’t responding to genuine problems but is emotionally reacting to loss of status.

“Men have most of the power” is rhetorically ambiguous

This phrase sounds straightforward:

“men have most of the power.”

But “men” can mean at least three different things:

  1. males as a biological population;
  2. men as a demographic group;
  3. individual men.

And “power” could mean:

  • political power;
  • corporate power;
  • economic resources;
  • physical power;
  • family power;
  • sexual power;
  • social status;
  • institutional authority.

Rickett moves between these meanings without specifying them.

That’s rhetorically powerful because the reader can interpret “men have most of the power” as a statement about society’s most powerful men while subconsciously applying it to ordinary men.

Compare Rickett’s language with Poole’s

The contrast within the same article is fascinating.

Rickett:

“true believers” “cult-like” “creepy” “woman-oppressors” “evil” “bunch of men” “frightened reaction”

Poole:

“important” “equality” “complex” “multi-faceted” “inequalities” “vitally important” “improving the lives of men and boys” “effective action”

Poole’s vocabulary is predominantly descriptive and policy-oriented.

Rickett’s is substantially more evaluative and derisive.

That doesn’t prove Poole’s arguments are correct and Rickett’s are wrong. But it tells us something about editorial framing: the two authors aren’t simply presenting competing evidence. They are assigning very different emotional statuses to the people whose interests they are discussing.

The most interesting loaded phrase: “frightened reaction”

If I had to pick one phrase as the most revealing, it would be:

“a frightened reaction to the perceived upturn in women’s fortunes”

It contains three rhetorical judgements in eight words:

  • “frightened” → these men are motivated by fear;
  • “reaction” → they aren’t initiating a legitimate movement; they’re reacting defensively;
  • “perceived upturn” → their understanding of the situation is subtly questioned.

Compare:

“a response to growing recognition of problems affecting men and boys”

That describes essentially the same phenomenon without assigning a psychological motive.

So the difference isn’t merely what facts are reported. It’s the lexical choices used to construct the moral and psychological character of the people involved.

And that is precisely the kind of framing that can make someone who identifies with the group being described feel that the article isn’t merely disagreeing with them - it is ridiculing and pathologising them before allowing them to make their case.

“someone who needs reassurance about his nationality waving his flag”

Again, male advocacy is associated with insecurity about identity.

The implication is that people involved in Men’s Day are not merely wrong; they’re pathetic people who enjoy being patronised.

A dignified man should remain silent rather than collectively complain about male problems.

4. The most revealing contrast: “real men”

There’s a fascinating contradiction in the comments.

Anti-Men’s Day:

“Real men don’t need it.”

“Any man who has need for such a day is not a man…”

Pro-Men’s Day:

“men can’t complain”

“man up”

These are actually talking about the same masculine norm.

The anti-Men’s Day commenters enforce the norm:

A real man doesn’t complain.

The pro-Men’s Day commenter criticises the norm:

Men are expected not to complain.

That is a very important distinction.

5. The comments reproduce the article’s central framing — but some challenge it

There’s a very clear linguistic divide.

Anti-MD vocabulary

“insecure” “creepy” “cult” “whiny” “whingy” “stupid” “shut up” “get on with it” “not a real man” “unfortunate people” “they have every day”

This is predominantly status-based and delegitimising.

Pro-MD vocabulary

“inequality” “discrimination” “stereotype” “victim” “underrepresented” “social pressure” “rights” “underperforming” “serious issues” “truth”

This is predominantly problem/rights-based.

Neutral/bridge-building vocabulary

“equality” “both” “people” “common” “differences” “issues” “social expectations” “both problems”

This is predominantly universalist or symmetrical.

6. And there’s an important asymmetry in the insults

The anti-Men’s Day comments repeatedly attack the character of men who want recognition:

insecure whiny not real men needy pathetic stupid

Whereas the strongest pro-Men’s Day comments generally attack ideas or social arrangements:

stereotype discrimination double standard lack of recognition

There are exceptions, of course. The pro-MD comments also contain inflammatory claims, particularly “guilty until proven innocent” and assertions about women being perpetrators. But the dominant rhetorical mode differs.

This is consistent with what we saw in Rickett’s article itself: the male claimant is sometimes treated as the object of analysis rather than as a legitimate claimant whose evidence should first be examined.

And perhaps the most revealing phrase across the entire set is:

“Real men don’t need it.”

Because it neatly demonstrates the problem from both sides. A person can oppose Men’s Day by saying men are already privileged, but they can also oppose it by saying a proper man shouldn’t need help or recognition.

The first is a political delegitimisation of men’s grievances; the second is a masculinity-based delegitimisation of the men expressing them. Both ultimately arrive at the same behavioural prescription:

Don’t complain about specifically male problems.

The phrase:

is especially useful for your broader analysis.

Men as a sex are structurally advantaged therefore an individual man cannot be disadvantaged on the basis of sex.

A society can have aggregate male advantages while particular males experience:

  • educational disadvantage;
  • occupational danger;
  • family separation;
  • violence;
  • suicide;
  • social isolation;
  • health disadvantages.

The rhetorical move is to treat group-level privilege as cancelling individual/group-specific disadvantage.

That’s precisely what “apex fallacy” is getting at.

This is a symmetry test.

The underlying question is:

Should a group’s aggregate social power determine whether that group’s specific disadvantages deserve attention?

If the answer is no, then men’s aggregate political/economic power doesn’t invalidate men’s issues.

If the answer is yes, then the same logic potentially undermines any women’s advocacy that focuses on disadvantages affecting women despite aggregate female advantages in some domains.

12. The really interesting finding

Across the comments, I think we can identify three competing conceptions of what it means to be a man.

Model 1 — The traditionalist

“Real men don’t need it.”

A proper man is stoic, self-sufficient and doesn’t complain.

Model 2 — The structuralist

“Men have most of the power.”

Men are structurally privileged, so male grievances must be interpreted against that background.

Model 3 — The egalitarian/men’s-issues position

“Men are people too.”

Men can simultaneously benefit from some forms of social privilege and suffer serious disadvantages in others.

What’s fascinating is that Models 1 and 2 disagree profoundly about why men’s complaints should be discounted, yet they can converge on the same conclusion: men’s complaints aren’t particularly legitimate.

That’s probably the strongest rhetorical pattern in this material.

Yes. This second batch makes the rhetorical pattern even clearer because you can see the pro- and anti-Men’s Day camps reacting to one another, and you can distinguish several recurring rhetorical techniques.

1. Dismissal through ridicule

A very common anti-IMD technique is to make the idea itself ridiculous rather than engage with the problems being raised:

  • “International Rescue Day … F.A.B.”
  • “International Love-Making Day”
  • “Be More Dog Day?”
  • “International Am I Bovvered Day?”
  • “Straight Pride”
  • “Should there be an International Goat Day? Yes!”
  • “Every day is men’s day waaaaah waaaaaaaah”

The repeated device is trivialisation: the proposed day is placed rhetorically alongside something absurd, childish or frivolous. The implicit argument is not “these particular male problems aren’t serious”, but “the very idea of having a day devoted to male problems is inherently silly.”

The laughter markers — “waaaaah”, “F.A.B.”, etc. — also frame supporters as emotionally immature complainers.

2. The “men already have everything” frame

This is probably the dominant argument:

“Every day is men’s day.”

and:

“In every aspect of life, in every part of the world, men hold most of the trump cards.”

and:

“Any group of people that is strong and thriving doesn’t need it.”

This substitutes group-level status for individual experience.

A man who is suicidal, homeless, imprisoned, struggling at school, abused by a partner, separated from his children, or killed in a workplace accident is rhetorically incorporated into the category “men have the power.”

That’s an important analytical distinction. The argument can be perfectly legitimate when discussing aggregate political power, but it doesn’t automatically answer whether particular male disadvantages deserve attention.

3. The apex-fallacy argument — and its mirror image

Several commenters explicitly notice this.

The pro-IMD side says:

“CEOs and prime ministers are men, therefore the ones at the bottom are delighting in their man-power year round!”

This is a criticism of reasoning from the status of highly successful men to the condition of ordinary men.

The counterargument is actually quite interesting because both sides can commit an analogous aggregation error.

For example:

“millionaires are overwhelmingly men. This proves the existence of a structural bias.”

That doesn’t follow automatically either. The fact that most millionaires are male tells us something about the distribution of wealth, but it doesn’t by itself establish why that distribution exists or that an individual ordinary man benefits from it.

So there’s a recurring rhetorical slippage:

male elites → ordinary men

and, sometimes:

female disadvantage → women collectively

Both are questionable inferences.

4. Collective guilt / collective responsibility

One particularly revealing example is:

“men take steps to deal with their own role in sexism issues”

followed by:

“the pressure that men face to become a victimizer, and silencer, of women.”

The male subject is implicitly constructed as someone who has a collective responsibility for male oppression of women.

Likewise:

“men … have had most of the power”

and:

“having run the World for millennia”

These formulations treat men as a fairly coherent historical agent.

The obvious problem is that “men” historically included kings and peasants, slaveholders and slaves, aristocrats and labourers, generals and conscripts, CEOs and unemployed men. Power was very unevenly distributed among men.

That doesn’t invalidate arguments about historical male dominance, but it makes the rhetoric much less precise than the underlying historical reality.

5. Moral status attached to the male category

There are some striking examples:

“embittered dinosaurs”

“MRA brigade”

“MRAs-under-the-bed delusion”

“hideout of ‘failures’”

“creeps and misogynists”

These are particularly significant because they don’t merely dispute particular arguments. They pre-classify the people making them.

A man raising male-specific problems can therefore be positioned as:

male grievance → MRA → misogyny → reactionary → potentially dangerous

That creates a rhetorical environment in which the speaker has to establish his moral legitimacy before his substantive argument can even be considered.

And you can see the counter-reaction:

“Fighting sexism with sexism isn’t helpful.”

and:

“This MRAs-under-the-bed delusion is getting rather tiresome.”

Those responses are essentially attacks on the delegitimisation mechanism itself.

6. “The legitimate problem / illegitimate movement” distinction

One of the more sophisticated anti-IMD arguments is:

“There are unquestionably issues that relate more to men than women that deserve a platform.”

followed by:

“populating the platform with the sort of embittered dinosaurs…”

This is rhetorically much stronger than simply saying “men don’t have problems.”

It concedes the underlying problems while attempting to separate the problems from the people claiming them.

The implication becomes:

Male problems are real, but the people who emphasise them are suspect.

That’s a potentially reasonable position — extremist movements really can contaminate legitimate causes — but it can also become a gatekeeping mechanism whereby almost any assertive advocacy gets classified as extremist.

7. “Women have it worse” as an implicit trump card

Consider:

“women and children tend to come off worst in wartime as well.”

and:

“There are no countries where the same happens to men because of theirs.”

The first statement may be defensible in some contexts, but rhetorically it functions as a comparative trump card:

Yes, men have problems, but women have worse problems.

That doesn’t logically establish that male problems shouldn’t receive attention.

A particularly revealing example is:

“rape crisis and domestic violence centers can’t scrape funding together to save women who are in actual physical danger”

followed by:

“a group too entitled to share their privilege.”

Here the argument moves from resource scarcity to moral entitlement. The male group isn’t merely asking for resources; it is characterised as taking resources that properly belong to women.

8. The “privilege” frame

This is particularly prominent:

“too entitled to share their privilege”

“the privileged and fortunate”

“having run the World for millennia”

The interesting feature of “privilege” rhetoric is that it can make an individual’s complaint evidence against the complaint.

If a man says:

“I’m disadvantaged in X.”

the response can effectively become:

“But you’re privileged as a man.”

Thus his claimed disadvantage doesn’t necessarily challenge the model; it can be interpreted as occurring within the model of male privilege.

That’s a potentially unfalsifiable rhetorical structure.

9. Gender essentialism — ironically from people opposing gender stereotypes

There’s an interesting contradiction here.

Anti-IMD commenters often reject male stereotypes while simultaneously making enormous generalisations about men:

“men have most of the power”

“Every day is men’s day.”

“every history lesson is a his-story lesson.”

“every news broadcast is a broadcast about what men are doing.”

“Nearly every company is run by men.”

The individual man disappears into a statistical abstraction called “men.”

Meanwhile, one of the stated purposes of IMD is precisely to examine the pressures imposed upon individual men by gender expectations.

10. The counter-rhetoric: “men are people too”

The strongest pro-IMD comments generally avoid the antagonistic rhetoric and make a much simpler argument:

“There are legitimate issues that men and boys face like the current state of boys education, suicide, child custody, unemployment, gang culture and domestic abuse.”

and:

“We really shouldn’t care [who runs companies], and looking after the home, the children … might result in improving awareness…”

and particularly:

“we’re not just providers of money, we’re people.”

This last one is rhetorically important. It rejects the idea that economic/political power is the sole measure of wellbeing.

11. Sarcasm is used by both sides

It’s worth noting that this isn’t simply anti-men vs pro-men. Both camps use ridicule.

Anti-IMD:

“Every day is men’s day waaaaah waaaaaaaah”

Pro-IMD:

“Every day is Vilify Men Day.”

Anti-IMD:

“Who will think of the great white male?”

Pro-IMD:

“MRA-under-the-bed delusion.”

And:

“Fighting sexism with sexism isn’t helpful.”

So the discussion progressively becomes a meta-conflict about whether the other side is prejudiced, rather than a discussion of the underlying empirical questions.

12. One particularly revealing rhetorical construction

This comment deserves special attention:

“An international womens day is needed, because discrimination has been targeted at one gender rather than at the other.”

That establishes a conceptual asymmetry:

Women’s discrimination = discrimination

Men’s disadvantage = something different, and therefore not necessarily discrimination.

Then:

“It is not our choice to understand ourselves as women.”

That introduces another argument: women’s identity is imposed by society, whereas a male-focused identity movement risks being voluntary grievance politics.

This is an ideological premise rather than an empirical conclusion.

  3. Therefore a special Men’s Day is unnecessary.

11. The most important rhetorical divide

Across these comments, I think there are actually four competing narratives, rather than merely two camps:

Narrative Basic claim Typical loaded language
Male privilege Men collectively possess social power, so IMD is unnecessary/suspicious “every day is men’s day”, “trump cards”, “privilege”, “patriarchy”
Male disadvantage Some serious male problems are ignored because they don’t fit the dominant equality narrative “invisible”, “pretending”, “brain washed”, “discriminated against”
Masculinity critique Men suffer because traditional masculine roles constrain them “trapped”, “real man”, “man up”, “victimizer”
Universalism Gender-specific days are themselves the problem “Gender Neutral Day”, “People’s Equality”, “be nice to people”, “human”

And there is a fifth position running through the discussion:

Men have legitimate problems, but the Men’s Rights movement is liable to express them in a misogynistic way.

That is probably the most sophisticated middle position represented in the comments.

One particularly revealing linguistic pattern

The most striking thing, to me, is the competing use of “men”.

When anti-IMD commenters say:

“Men have the power”

“men destroy other men”

“Every day is men’s day”

men functions as a structural category.

When pro-IMD commenters say:

“men have higher suicide rates”

“men are victims of violent crime”

“men are discriminated against”

men functions as a demographic category.

And when commenters discuss:

“real men”

“man up”

“traditional roles”

men functions as a cultural/psychological category.

A great deal of the apparent disagreement therefore comes from shifting meanings of the word “men” without explicitly acknowledging the shift.

That is probably the most important rhetorical feature of the entire comment corpus.

1. Broad position of the comments

The comments divide roughly into these camps:

Position Characteristic argument
Strongly pro-IMD Men face serious, under-recognised problems and need a public platform

The important thing is that “pro-Men’s Day” does not mean one coherent ideology. Some commenters want men’s issues addressed without feminism; some explicitly want men’s issues addressed through feminism; others simply want a universalist approach.

2. The strongest pro-IMD argument: male disadvantage as evidence

DazzyBoy provides probably the most concentrated example in this batch.

“96% of workplace deaths, men. Worse health of both genders, men. Least gender specific health funding, men. 85% of prison inmates, men. 75% of suicides, males. 99% of war deaths, men. 80% of victims of violence, men. Most unemployed, men. 80% of homeless, men. Most drug, alcohol and gambling addicts, men.”

This is enumerative rhetoric: a long succession of statistics creates cumulative force.

The technique is important because the individual figures don’t need to be examined rhetorically one by one. The sheer accumulation produces the conclusion:

There is obviously a substantial male-problem category.

It is also a deliberate counter to the common argument:

Men have power.

The implied response is:

Which men?

That is one of the most important recurring tensions in the entire thread: aggregate male privilege versus male disadvantage at the bottom of society.

DazzyBoy explicitly resolves it through class:

“It is not a gender war, it is, always has been and always will be a class war.”

That is a significant move. Rather than denying that men are disproportionately represented among powerful people, he argues that powerful men and ordinary men should not be treated as one social group.

Sandydog immediately challenges one of the statistics:

“I don’t know where you come up with 99% of war deaths being men.”

This is an interesting counter-rhetorical move.

Rather than arguing that male disadvantage doesn’t exist, the commenter attacks the credibility of the evidential package.

That distinction matters.

The argument becomes:

Some of these problems are real versus the particular collection of statistics you’re using is unreliable or exaggerated.

So the dispute isn’t necessarily over the existence of male problems; it is over who gets to define and quantify them.

Framework B

Men suffer because traditional gender roles harm men as well as women.

WordTorrent explicitly adopts B:

This is a powerful rhetorical strategy because it appropriates the evidence offered by the opposing side.

That’s a much more sophisticated disagreement.

Yes. This batch is especially useful because it contains several direct clashes over evidence, and it also exposes a recurring distinction between “men have problems” and “men are discriminated against.”

1. The discussion is becoming more empirical

The earlier comments often argued at the level of principle:

“Men have power.”

“Men need a voice.”

“Every day is men’s day.”

Here, commenters increasingly try to establish their positions with specific statistics.

RichRich produces a long list:

  • male life expectancy
  • workplace deaths
  • prison population
  • youth unemployment
  • suicide
  • university attendance
  • male teachers and nurses
  • media portrayals
  • criminal sentencing
  • rape law
  • violence
  • homelessness

This changes the nature of the argument.

Instead of:

Should men have a day?

the argument becomes:

Are men actually disadvantaged in these particular domains?

And then:

If so, why?

That second question is where the real disagreement lies.

2. The statistics become almost a form of “counter-privilege accounting”

RichRich’s list is effectively an attempt to construct a male disadvantage balance sheet.

The implicit argument is:

People keep telling us about women’s disadvantages. Here is the corresponding list for men.

This is rhetorically very similar to the feminist practice of documenting:

  • pay gaps
  • political representation
  • violence against women
  • reproductive rights
  • workplace discrimination
  • etc.

The difference is that the male list emphasises outcomes in which men are overwhelmingly represented:

death, imprisonment, suicide, homelessness, unemployment, violence and educational underachievement.

This is arguably the clearest recurring pro-IMD strategy in the whole discussion:

If equality campaigns are legitimate when they document group disparities affecting women, why shouldn’t they be legitimate when documenting disparities affecting men?

3. But the opposition attacks the leap from disparity to discrimination

This is probably the most important distinction emerging from this batch.

RichRich says:

“In education boys are discriminated against”

“In the family courts men are discriminated against.”

“In prison men are treated more harshly.”

But TheMackenator responds:

“Alot of these things are driven by the behaviour of men and boys, not any overarching discriminatory system or campaign.”

That’s a fundamentally different model.

Model A — discrimination

Male disadvantage → institutional bias against men

Model B — behavioural/social causes

Male disadvantage → behaviour, choices, social circumstances, etc.

Model C — structural gender norms

Male disadvantage → traditional gender roles / masculinity

These three explanations recur throughout the corpus.

And importantly, the same statistic doesn’t tell you which explanation is correct.

A male prison population of 95%, for example, doesn’t by itself demonstrate anti-male discrimination.

Nor does it demonstrate that discrimination is absent.

You need additional evidence about sentencing, offending, judicial behaviour, policing, legislation, etc.

4. This is where the “correlation ≠ cause” issue becomes central

The homelessness exchange is a good example.

BabyGenius:

“Homelessness - vast majority of homeless people are men”

Unbanned:

“Homelessness is caused by poverty, not gender.”

BabyGenius doesn’t actually need to claim that homelessness is caused by being male.

The relevant claim could instead be:

Homelessness disproportionately affects men, therefore a men’s advocacy programme might reasonably address it.

Those are different propositions.

This distinction is often lost in the discussion.

Proposition 1

Men are disproportionately homeless.

Proposition 2

Being male causes homelessness.

Proposition 3

Something about male socialisation contributes to male homelessness.

Proposition 4

Homeless services should therefore have male-specific components.

These are four separate claims.

The thread often jumps from 1 directly to 2 or 4.

5. The circumcision example is different

BabyGenius identifies circumcision as:

“exclusively affects men”

That is a much stronger case for a male-specific issue than homelessness.

But even here, the disagreement shifts from:

Does this disproportionately affect males?

to:

Is it actually a problem?

Unbanned argues circumcision is:

“applied to men for health reasons largely”

and frames it as cultural/parental choice.

So again, there are two separate questions:

  1. Who is affected?
  2. Should society regard the practice as harmful or discriminatory?

A demographic statistic can answer the first without answering the second.

6. The healthcare argument is particularly revealing

RichRich says:

“Men die on average 4 years before women.”

and:

“Least gender specific health funding, men.”

and argues that resources should be shifted toward increasing male life expectancy.

This is an interesting example of outcome-based reasoning:

Men have worse health outcomes → resources should be redistributed toward men.

But there’s an obvious counterargument:

Different health outcomes do not necessarily imply discrimination in healthcare.

Men may have different rates of:

  • smoking
  • drinking
  • occupational exposure
  • dangerous employment
  • risk-taking
  • delayed medical consultation
  • particular diseases

etc.

So again:

disparity ≠ discrimination.

But equally:

disparity does not prove the absence of discrimination either.

The interesting empirical question is what proportion of the difference is attributable to each factor.

7. The gender-role explanation is becoming increasingly prominent

MsJess gives one of the more nuanced comments:

“society paints women as ‘naturally’ nurturing and men not.”

She then argues:

“Men and women are getting the shit end of the stick with regards to entrenched traditional gender roles.”

This is a significant middle position.

It accepts:

  • male disadvantage
  • female disadvantage

while rejecting the idea that these necessarily require opposing political camps.

The proposed solution is essentially:

de-gender social roles.

For example:

  • father as primary caregiver shouldn’t be regarded as unusual
  • woman who isn’t naturally good with children shouldn’t be regarded as defective
  • men shouldn’t necessarily be breadwinners
  • women shouldn’t necessarily be caregivers

This is probably the most genuinely symmetrical gender-equality argument appearing in the thread.

Another recurring distinction is becoming clearer.

TheMackenator says:

“a day dedicated to raising awareness of things that relate to men … can’t hurt.”

But irussell asks whether these days actually accomplish anything.

This separates:

The substantive question

Are there important problems affecting men?

from:

The institutional question

Is an International Men’s Day an effective intervention?

A person can answer:

Yes to the first and no to the second.

That’s an important position because many commenters seem to assume that denying the usefulness of Men’s Day means denying men’s problems.

It doesn’t.

Men’s Day should be a vehicle for specific campaigns, not merely a celebration of maleness.

And that is very close to the position of some of the more reasonable commenters in the previous batches.

10. The “everybody day” argument is a universalist escape hatch

Unbanned proposes:

“an international everybody day”

and the earlier comments propose:

“International Gender Neutral Day”

and:

“International Human Rights Day.”

These arguments are attractive because they avoid the problem of competing identity groups.

Instead of:

women vs men

the principle becomes:

individuals have rights regardless of group membership.

Platform3 makes this explicit:

“An individual man who finds himself powerless deserves his lot because he is merely part of his gender group”

That’s actually one of the stronger philosophical objections to group-based identity politics in the corpus.

11. Platform3 identifies an important problem with aggregate statistics

This is one of the best comments analytically:

“So it’s all about averages is it?”

The commenter objects to treating:

men as a powerful group

as though this tells us whether an individual man possesses power.

That’s a legitimate statistical point.

Suppose men occupy 70% of senior positions.

That tells us something about the distribution of positions.

It doesn’t tell us:

“This particular man is powerful.”

Likewise:

95% of prisoners are men

doesn’t mean:

“Being male makes this particular man more likely to be criminal.”

The thread repeatedly confuses group-level statistics with individual-level explanations.

12. OirishMartin takes this further

He says:

“Appealing to averages is in effect no different from stereotypical behaviour.”

That’s rhetorically clever.

A stereotype is essentially:

People belonging to group X are like this.

An aggregate statistical statement is:

People belonging to group X are, on average, more likely to be like this.

The second is empirically legitimate when supported by data, whereas the first may be prejudicial.

But there is still a philosophical similarity:

both move from group characteristics toward assumptions about individuals.

The distinction between statistical generalisation and stereotyping therefore matters enormously.

13. The thread contains a particularly important argument about “collective guilt”

Platform3 says:

“Men’s and Women’s days will always be more about collective guilt than the plight of the individual.”

That’s a very useful way of understanding some of the hostility.

Some commenters hear:

“Men have power.”

as:

You personally are implicated in the actions of powerful men.

Others hear:

“Men suffer disproportionately.”

as:

You personally are being blamed for problems you didn’t cause.

This creates a psychological resistance to group-level discourse.

The counterargument is that group-level policy is often unavoidable. Public-health campaigns, anti-discrimination legislation, educational policy and epidemiology all work with populations.

So the real question isn’t:

“Should we ever talk about groups?”

It is:

How do we talk about group-level disparities without assigning collective guilt to individuals?

That’s a much stronger formulation.

14. There is also a very interesting “men are both perpetrators and victims” problem

JimNolan quotes:

“men and boys account for more than 80% of violent deaths worldwide”

and asks what “account for” means.

Unbanned responds:

“they both suffer them, and cause them.”

This is an important point that appears repeatedly.

Men can simultaneously be:

  • disproportionately perpetrators of violence
  • disproportionately victims of violence

There is no logical contradiction.

This is particularly important because one side often treats male perpetration as evidence that men’s problems aren’t legitimate, while the other treats male victimisation as evidence that feminism has ignored men.

Both can coexist.

15. The strongest example of “male disadvantage” is arguably suicide

Across virtually all these comment threads, male suicide is one of the least contested examples.

Even commenters who reject Men’s Day generally don’t say:

Men don’t have a suicide problem.

Instead they debate:

  • why men die more often
  • whether masculinity contributes
  • unemployment
  • help-seeking
  • social isolation
  • healthcare
  • methodology

That makes suicide a useful bridge issue.

You don’t need to establish that:

“society discriminates against men”

in order to establish that:

men have a disproportionately serious suicide problem that deserves intervention.

That distinction could have substantially lowered the temperature of the debate.

16. The “angry men” argument introduces a pragmatic reason for action

RichRich says:

“Many men are angry at the way society discriminates against them.”

and:

“It will cost society dearly unless something is done”

This is not primarily a moral argument.

It’s a stability argument:

Even if you don’t sympathise with these men, ignoring their grievances may produce political or social consequences.

That is an interesting argument because it doesn’t require accepting every grievance as valid.

It says:

There is a politically alienated male population; understanding and addressing legitimate grievances may be socially prudent.

This becomes particularly relevant given the later line:

“These men and boys will be picked up by fringe political parties.”

That’s an important recurring concern in the corpus.

17. The thread repeatedly struggles to distinguish three things

I think this is becoming the central analytical framework for the entire corpus.

A. Male-specific problems

Things that physically or statistically affect men disproportionately.

Examples:

  • prostate/testicular disease
  • male suicide
  • workplace deaths
  • male homelessness
  • male imprisonment
  • circumcision
B. Problems caused partly by male socialisation

Examples:

  • reluctance to seek help
  • emotional suppression
  • risk-taking
  • violence
  • difficulty forming support networks
C. Anti-male discrimination

Examples claimed by commenters:

  • sentencing differences
  • family courts
  • university admissions
  • health funding
  • employment programmes
  • gender-specific recruitment

These categories are not interchangeable.

A man can suffer from A without suffering from C.

And C can exist even when A isn’t present.

Some of the worst arguments in the thread collapse all three into:

“Men have problems because society discriminates against men.”

Others collapse them into:

“Men have problems because men behave badly.”

Both are overly simple.

18. The most balanced position emerging from the thread

There is a surprisingly coherent position buried underneath all the noise:

Men demonstrably experience some distinctive and disproportionate problems. Those problems deserve attention. But the existence of male disadvantage does not automatically demonstrate anti-male discrimination, nor does it require hostility toward feminism. A useful Men’s Day would therefore focus on concrete problems and interventions rather than collective grievance.

That position accommodates:

  • BabyGenius
  • TheMackenator
  • MsJess
  • OttoMaddox
  • BlackberryBlossom
  • some of groucho1980’s comments
  • parts of WordTorrent’s position

while rejecting the more sweeping claims of both extremes.

19. And the biggest recurring rhetorical error

Across all four batches now, I’d identify one particularly important pattern:

Participants frequently treat a descriptive statistic as though it were a causal or moral argument.

For example:

“Most prisoners are men.”

is descriptive.

It does not tell us whether:

  • men are inherently more criminal,
  • men are discriminated against,
  • masculinity produces criminality,
  • policing discriminates against men,
  • sentencing discriminates against men,
  • socioeconomic conditions explain the disparity,
  • or some combination of these.

Likewise:

“Men occupy most positions of power.”

doesn’t establish:

“individual men are privileged.”

And:

“Men die younger.”

doesn’t establish:

“women receive discriminatory healthcare.”

The empirical question always needs an additional step:

What mechanism produces the disparity?

That is where this Guardian discussion is weakest.

20. One particularly useful conclusion from this batch

The debate is actually much more sophisticated when people stop asking:

“Are men oppressed?”

and start asking:

“Which male outcomes are worse, for which men, by how much, and why?”

That formulation avoids the enormous conceptual trap of treating “men” as a single social class.

It also allows several things to be true simultaneously:

  • some men are extraordinarily powerful;
  • some men are extraordinarily disadvantaged;
  • men can be both perpetrators and victims;
  • traditional masculinity can harm men;
  • some institutions can discriminate against men;
  • women can simultaneously experience serious disadvantages;
  • and none of these propositions logically cancels the others.

That, to me, is the most valuable analytical insight emerging from the whole comment corpus so far.

Exegesis

Yes. This comment section is particularly useful because the rhetoric is more varied than the two authors’ pieces. You have outright mockery, anti-feminist counter-mockery, apparently neutral universalism, substantive men’s advocacy, and a particularly interesting form of rhetoric in which men’s problems are acknowledged but immediately converted into men’s responsibility for their own behaviour.

I would analyse it in several layers.

1. There are roughly five rhetorical camps

The comments seem to fall into these broad rhetorical positions:

A. Mockery / trivialisation of men’s advocacy

Examples:

  • “go to the pub”
  • “I believe it is your round”
  • “Shrove’s”
  • “poor Shroves”
  • “up the Y chromosome”
  • “posing pouches”
  • “nearest Wetherspoons”
  • “male version of feminism … huge penis”

These don’t primarily argue against men’s issues. They make the idea of men’s collective political identity ridiculous.

B. Universalist egalitarianism

Examples:

  • “dispense with both men and women days”
  • “human day”
  • “people in general”
  • “problems … exist all year round”

These commenters aren’t necessarily hostile to men. Their argument is:

Sex-specific categorisation itself is counterproductive.

This is a much more intellectually respectable objection than simple mockery.

C. Men’s-issues advocacy

Examples:

  • MsJess
  • pangar
  • gingerjon
  • thebigchil
  • PhilWright774

These comments attempt to name concrete male-specific problems rather than simply complain about feminism.

D. Feminist structural response

Examples:

  • Visubverted
  • Atavism
  • Goldendays49

Here the basic framework is:

Men remain structurally advantaged, therefore male-specific grievance requires suspicion or should be subordinated to women’s problems.

E. Anti-feminist counterattack

Examples:

  • PhilWright774
  • parts of the 100qindarka/Ronan exchange
  • Goldendays49, although his comment is particularly extreme

Here men’s advocacy becomes an opportunity to attack feminism.

The interesting thing is that the article itself explicitly asks whether International Men’s Day will be about men’s problems or about opposition to feminism. The comments demonstrate both possibilities.

2. The pub jokes are more rhetorically significant than they initially appear

The opening exchange is:

“Throw away those shackles, brother, and go to the pub.”

“I believe it is your round.”

Then:

“I am ideologically opposed to gender politics, I propose we have a Babycham.”

This is clever because it constructs a caricature of male liberation.

The implied equation is:

men’s rights → male bonding → drinking → football → irresponsibility

The joke isn’t merely “International Men’s Day is silly.”

It’s:

What could men possibly want to liberate themselves from? Their natural habitat is already the pub.

That is a form of status inversion. The supposedly oppressed male is portrayed as a person whose great political demand is to go drinking with his mates.

The “Babycham” response then deliberately exaggerates this stereotype. Babycham is a humorous pseudo-masculine drink, so the commenter is essentially saying:

“If we’re going to do stereotypical masculinity, let’s make it absurd.”

3. The Shrove Tuesday exchange is actually a very good piece of rhetorical technique

This sequence:

“Shrove Tuesday isn’t exclusively for Shrove’s.”

followed by:

“Aha another anti-Shrovian.”

“Those poor Shroves, huddled up on the last Tuesday they have…”

is reductio ad absurdum through analogy.

The commenter takes the proposition:

“A day doesn’t belong exclusively to the demographic it names”

and applies it to Shrove Tuesday.

Then they personify “the Shroves”:

“those poor Shroves”

“cruelly pushed off”

“deep abyss of daylessness”

This deliberately over-literalises the concept of a named day.

It’s effective because it exposes the possible absurdity of treating International Men’s Day as though naming a day for men somehow deprives everyone else of that day.

It is also a good example of mock solidarity:

“I’m weeping as I type.”

The speaker pretends to mourn the fictional oppression of the Shroves.

That is rhetorically similar to political satire: an argument is made by pretending to adopt its logic to an obviously ridiculous case.

4. Pangar is rhetorically different: narrative legitimisation

Pangar’s comment is one of the more interesting ones:

“Most of those schools were as rough as hell.”

“barrel chested and bored boys”

“bravado spewing”

Then the reversal:

“What really surprised [me] is who … boys were truly wary of.”

The rhetorical technique is personal testimony followed by reversal of expectation.

The expected story is:

rough boys intimidate everyone.

The revealed story is:

rough boys are themselves intimidated by socially sophisticated girls.

That allows Pangar to construct boys as simultaneously:

  • physically imposing;
  • socially immature;
  • vulnerable;
  • disadvantaged by their lack of linguistic/social skills.

The phrase:

“teenage boys often get a raw deal a few times over”

is particularly interesting.

“Raw deal” is colloquial rather than statistical. It makes the argument morally intuitive rather than empirically quantified.

And:

“doing more for them is a genuine investment”

changes the frame from sympathy to social utility.

Boys aren’t merely victims deserving help; helping them benefits society.

That’s a powerful rhetorical move because it avoids the potentially politically vulnerable language of “male grievance.”

5. MsJess is probably the strongest example of constructive men’s advocacy

Her comment is notable because it is almost entirely concrete nouns and proposed actions:

  • eating disorders;
  • rape;
  • domestic violence;
  • prostate cancer;
  • testicular cancer;
  • paternity leave;
  • care professions;
  • mental health.

There is very little ideological language.

She doesn’t say:

“Men are oppressed.”

She says:

Here are things that could be done.

That gives her comment a very different rhetorical character from many of the others.

Even more importantly, she explicitly says:

“It could be used for good”

and:

“Instead it usually seems to be a reason to complain about feminism or women in general.”

So she pre-emptively distances men’s advocacy from anti-feminism.

That’s significant given the surrounding discussion. She’s effectively saying:

“I’m not asking you to choose men over women. I’m asking you to take these particular problems seriously.”

6. The “human day” argument is rhetorically attractive but contains a hidden assumption

GoogleWhack says:

“Problems that face men and women are not exclusive”

and:

“we could work together to do good for both.”

This uses the language of universal humanism.

Then:

“human day”

is presented as the logical culmination.

The rhetorical appeal is obvious:

Why divide humanity when we can unite it?

But MsJess identifies the practical objection:

“would anything get done though?”

This is an important distinction between moral universalism and political organisation.

“Everyone matters” doesn’t necessarily tell you:

Who should be targeted by this intervention?

If males have a substantially higher suicide rate, a campaign specifically designed to reach men isn’t necessarily opposed to universal mental-health care.

This is where MsJess’s phrase is particularly important:

“targeted campaigns as long as they’re not shitting on other people”

The profanity is doing useful rhetorical work here. It compresses her entire position into a memorable rule:

Targeting is fine; antagonism isn’t.

That’s probably one of the clearest formulations in the entire comment section.

7. GoogleWhack then performs a subtle universalist manoeuvre

GoogleWhack responds:

“women do as well”

after MsJess points out higher male suicide rates.

This is rhetorically interesting.

It doesn’t deny the statistic.

Instead, it redirects attention from the magnitude of the difference to the commonality of the problem.

The structure is:

Men have higher suicide rates.

becomes:

Women commit suicide too.

Therefore:

We should campaign for everyone.

This is an example of universalising a gendered disparity.

It isn’t necessarily wrong. But it does have the effect of making the sex differential rhetorically disappear.

The same happens with paternity leave:

“we should be helping both sexes”

Again, the argument is:

Different sexes experience the issue → therefore address the common human problem.

But that doesn’t answer the separate question:

Are there sex-specific barriers or outcomes requiring sex-specific advocacy?

8. Atavism uses “we’ve already got it pretty damn easy” as moral compression

This is a very interesting phrase:

“We’ve already got it pretty damn easy compared to women”

It compresses an enormous empirical argument into one colloquial judgement.

Then:

“we can stand on our own two feet”

is metaphorical.

It implies:

  • men aren’t vulnerable;
  • men aren’t dependent;
  • men don’t need special assistance;
  • requesting it would be weakness or favour-seeking.

Then comes:

“extra favouritism and advantage”

Notice what has happened.

International Men’s Day has been transformed from:

a campaign about male-specific problems

into:

a request for additional privilege.

That’s a significant rhetorical reframing.

The word “extra” is especially important because it presupposes that the male population already receives enough—or excessive—benefits.

9. Gingerjon’s response is rhetorically very effective

He quotes:

“We’ve already got it pretty damn easy compared to women”

and responds:

“Yes, aside from the higher suicide rates, earlier deaths, higher rates of drug use, higher rates of criminality, lower rates of education success etc.”

The opening:

“Yes, aside from…”

is sarcastic concession.

It means:

“I’ll grant you your proposition, provided we ignore a large number of inconvenient facts.”

Then comes the list.

This is counter-list rhetoric.

Atavism has provided a broad claim.

Gingerjon responds with a series of concrete indicators that complicate it.

Then he delivers the strongest sentence:

“Don’t let the fact that most powerful people are men lead you to believe that most men have power.”

This is almost a concise formulation of the entire analytical problem we’ve been discussing.

It distinguishes:

men as occupants of elite positions

from:

men as an ordinary population.

Rhetorically, it’s strong because it takes the opponent’s own premise—“powerful people are mostly men”—and says:

You’re committing a logical inference that doesn’t follow.

10. Goldendays49 uses an entirely different rhetorical register

This comment:

“The answer to the anti-social growth of feminism is not for men to start a similar ‘me, me, me’ organisation.”

is overtly ideological.

The phrase:

“me, me, me”

infantilises male advocacy.

It reframes demands for recognition as selfishness.

Then:

“Let women isolate themselves if they want to”

and:

“just don’t let them back in.”

This escalates into deliberate exclusionary rhetoric.

The interesting part is the follow-up:

“So who are the gatekeepers of society, then?”

“A rattling good point.”

The second response doesn’t retreat from the authoritarian implication. It embraces it ironically.

So this little exchange is essentially political theatre:

Speaker A: proposes exclusion.

Speaker B: exposes the implication.

Speaker A: accepts the implication.

That’s much closer to trolling or provocation than substantive argument.

11. Visubverted uses “men should fix men” as a rhetorical trap

This is one of the most important patterns in the thread.

Visubverted says:

“Since men cause most violence…”

“these may be problems for men themselves to look at in terms of their own behaviour”

and:

“Maybe this could be a day for men to think about getting their acts together?”

This changes the category of the problem.

Instead of:

Men experience X disproportionately

it becomes:

Men cause X disproportionately.

That’s a crucial rhetorical substitution.

For example:

Male suicide is an outcome experienced disproportionately by men.

But the commenter’s framework encourages the reader to think:

men are responsible for bad things men do.

That might be relevant to violence, but it doesn’t obviously apply to:

  • prostate cancer;
  • testicular cancer;
  • male suicide;
  • boys’ educational difficulties.

The response from PhilWright774 catches this:

“Are you applying that to the likes of male suicide, prostate cancer…”

He’s exposing a category mismatch.

12. “Whinging inaction” is a particularly revealing insult

Visubverted later says:

“whinging inaction”

This is rhetorically interesting because it combines:

  • whinging → they’re complaining rather than reasoning;
  • inaction → they’re hypocrites who don’t actually do anything.

So male advocacy is simultaneously portrayed as:

complaining + laziness + entitlement.

That’s a powerful delegitimisation combination.

And notice the irony:

MsJess has just given an extensive list of possible actions.

So the accusation of “inaction” isn’t actually supported by the preceding comment. It is directed at an imagined class of men’s-rights activists.

That’s another example of stereotyping the movement rather than responding to the particular argument being made.

13. 100qindarka uses “responsibility rhetoric”

This comment is especially revealing:

“What have men been doing about addressing the causes of those statistics?”

Then the list:

“being less violent”

“driving more carefully”

“smoking less”

“taking more exercise”

“working harder at school”

“feeling less sorry for themselves”

“being better fathers and husbands”

The rhetorical structure is:

statistic → presumed male behaviour → moral responsibility.

The list deliberately mixes:

Potentially behavioural causes
  • violence
  • dangerous driving
  • smoking
  • exercise

with:

Normative judgements
  • “working harder”
  • “feeling less sorry for themselves”
  • “being better fathers and husbands”

The latter are particularly revealing because they aren’t simply descriptions of statistical causation. They’re moral prescriptions.

So the statistics become evidence for a broader moral indictment of men.

14. Ronan’s “asking for it” response exposes the implication

Ronan says:

“Exactly. They’re just ‘asking for it’ aren’t they…….”

This is sarcasm.

He invokes the familiar victim-blaming phrase “asking for it”, normally associated with arguments about women and sexual violence.

He’s effectively saying:

If this reasoning is acceptable for men, it resembles the very victim-blaming logic that is normally condemned when applied to women.

This is rhetorically clever because it forces the opponent’s argument into a familiar moral category.

15. 100qindarka responds by escalating into a stereotype of failed masculinity

The response is much more personal:

“there’s no woman at home looking after the children, doing the shopping, cleaning the house, cooking his dinner and saying how wonderful a man he is.”

This is extraordinary because the commenter is now imagining a particular type of man:

  • dependent on women;
  • domestically incompetent;
  • narcissistic;
  • emotionally needy;
  • patriarchal;
  • resentful.

Then:

“he’s failed his boys”

and:

“condemn them to the same kind of miserable life”

The rhetorical strategy has moved completely away from the original empirical claims.

It is now a moral narrative about defective masculinity.

In other words:

male disadvantage → men’s own bad behaviour → defective male socialisation → dysfunctional fathers.

That’s a much more comprehensive ideological story than the evidence presented actually establishes.

16. The “asking for it” exchange is probably the most revealing part

Because you get a miniature demonstration of two competing explanatory frameworks.

Framework 1

Men have worse outcomes because men engage in risky/bad behaviour.

Therefore:

men need to change.

Framework 2

Men have worse outcomes partly because institutions/social expectations fail to address male-specific needs.

Therefore:

society needs to change too.

These are not mutually exclusive.

Men can be responsible for some behaviours and institutions can inadequately address male-specific problems.

But the comments tend to treat them as competing explanations.

That’s important for your broader analysis.

17. The “equalism” comment is unusually sophisticated

Thebigchil says:

“I’d love there to be a new ‘ism’ (maybe ‘equalism’?)”

This is interesting because the commenter explicitly refuses the binary:

feminism versus anti-feminism.

Instead:

equalism = issues affecting men + issues affecting women.

And importantly:

“alongside the equally important and undoubtedly more numerous issues that affect women.”

That concession is rhetorically strategic.

It says:

I’m not claiming men suffer more. I’m claiming their problems should be included.

This makes the argument considerably harder to attack as simple anti-feminism.

18. The comments also reveal a distinction between two kinds of “equality”

This might be the most useful conceptual distinction in the entire section.

Universal equality

Treat everyone the same.

This is GoogleWhack’s position.

Substantive/targeted equality

If different groups experience different problems, sometimes address them differently.

This is MsJess’s position.

The disagreement isn’t necessarily about whether equality is desirable.

It’s about whether treating everyone identically produces equal outcomes.

That’s the same conceptual problem that appears repeatedly in debates about gender.

19. Humour itself is functioning asymmetrically

A lot of the anti-IMD comments use humour:

  • pub
  • Babycham
  • Shrove’s
  • Wetherspoons
  • huge penis
  • World Toilet Day
  • carbonated beverage day

But humour isn’t neutral.

It can perform status work.

The repeated joke is:

Men asking for recognition is inherently funny.

That’s subtly different from:

“I disagree with the policy.”

The first attacks the social legitimacy of the claimant.

That’s why the humour matters analytically.

20. Conversely, some pro-male commenters also use humour—but defensively

Compare:

“Don’t let the fact that most powerful people are men lead you to believe that most men have power.”

That’s comparatively serious.

Whereas:

“They’re just ‘asking for it’ aren’t they…”

uses humour/sarcasm to expose what the commenter sees as hypocrisy.

So there’s an interesting asymmetry:

Anti-IMD humour tends to make male advocacy itself ridiculous.

Pro-IMD humour tends to attack the reasoning used against male advocacy.

Those are rhetorically different functions.

21. The deepest recurring rhetorical pattern

Across these comments, I’d identify this sequence:

Men’s problem is raised

↓

Response A:

Universalise it

“Women have this problem too.”

↓

Response B:

Moralise it

“Men cause this problem themselves.”

↓

Response C:

Structuralise it

“Men are already the powerful group.”

↓

Response D:

Ridicule it

“Pub! Beer! Football! Y chromosome!”

The common feature is that the original proposition—

“This particular problem disproportionately affects men”

—is rarely directly disputed.

Instead, commenters frequently change the question.

They move from:

Does this happen?

to:

Is it legitimate to call attention to it?

or:

Whose fault is it?

or:

Why should men receive special attention when women also suffer?

or:

What does male advocacy mean politically?

Those are different questions.

And that’s probably the most important thing to preserve when analysing this thread.

The strongest comments in the thread—particularly MsJess, Pangar, gingerjon and thebigchil—tend to keep the discussion at the level of specific problems and proposed solutions. The weaker comments tend to move rapidly from male-specific outcome to male moral character, male collective power, or ridicule.

That distinction could be useful as a general framework for analysing the rest of the Guardian thread.

The commenter isn’t particularly arguing that Men’s Day is good in itself. Rather, the argument is:

If male-only discussion is illegitimate, the same principle should apply to female-only discussion.

That’s a symmetry argument.

“Here it’s just more literally bollocks” then makes the anatomical pun explicit.
