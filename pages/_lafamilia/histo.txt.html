Analysis

Your histopathology report indicates In Situ Squamous Cell Carcinoma (IEC)—often referred to as Bowen’s disease—in both of the biopsied punch sites on your right hand dorsum and your left AC joint area, with noted hair follicle involvement.

Understanding the Findings

  • In Situ Squamous Cell Carcinoma (IEC): This is a very common, non-invasive form of skin cancer where abnormal squamous cells are confined entirely to the epidermis (the outermost layer of skin). Because it is in situ, it has not penetrated into deeper layers and has no potential to spread internally at this stage.
  • Hair Follicle Involvement: The report notes that the changes extend down into the hair follicles. This is a common finding in sun-exposed areas and is important for your treating doctor or dermatologist to consider when planning the most effective removal method to ensure complete clearance.

Next Steps & Management While an in-situ diagnosis is not an emergency, it does require definitive treatment to prevent it from eventually progressing into an invasive squamous cell carcinoma over time. Management options for IEC typically depend on the size, depth, and exact location of the lesions, and can include options such as surgical excision, curettage and cautery, topical therapies, or destructive treatments like cryotherapy.

Active surveillance for in situ squamous cell carcinoma (Bowen’s disease) means closely monitoring the lesions for changes rather than removing them immediately.

What Surveillance Involves

  • Monitoring for Progression: The core goal is watching to see if the superficial abnormal cells remain contained within the outer layer of skin or if they begin to show signs of growing thicker, changing texture, or developing into an invasive squamous cell carcinoma.
  • Regular Clinical Checks: This approach requires routine skin checks—often with clinical photography to track size and borders—so that if the lesion does begin to change, it can be treated promptly before it goes deeper.

Can It Disappear On Its Own?

  • Spontaneous Regression: It is exceedingly rare for an established in situ squamous cell carcinoma to completely and permanently resolve on its own. While minor surface inflammation or scaling might fluctuate, the underlying atypical cellular changes typically persist.
  • The Risk of Waiting: Unlike some benign spots or certain types of superficial skin changes that can occasionally fade, untreated in situ lesions carry a cumulative risk over time of evolving into invasive skin cancer.
